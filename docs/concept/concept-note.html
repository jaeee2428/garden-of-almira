Concept note · draft v0.2 · October 2026

Hardin ni Almira

A cozy deduction game set in a Cebuano seaside garden. Lola Almira, a botanist who is going blind, has kept a journal with a poem and her knowledge for each of her flowers. As her sight fades, the pages are left unfinished. You become her eyes: find each flower, describe it to her, and finish the journal before her knowledge is lost.

Princess Jaena Marie O. De La Peña · BS Computer Science, UP Cebu · Special Problem
Working title, inspired by the title of Erlinda K. Alburo's Sinug-ang Hardin ni Almira. Content from the poems will only be used with her permission.

1. Vision

The problem

Knowledge about Cebuano plants, like their names, their folk uses and the stories told about them, mostly lives with elders and gets passed on by word of mouth. Younger Cebuanos often know these flowers by sight but not by name, and seldom know what they were used for.

The idea

A browser-based 3D garden where you learn by noticing and working things out, not by reading a list. Each flower is a small mystery. Solving it fills a page of your herbarium journal and brings back a memory, a word and a story.

Design pillars

🔍

Observe, then deduce

Every answer can be found in the world: scent at night, leaf shape, where it grows, who uses it. No quest markers.

📖

The journal is the game

Progress lives in a hand-drawn herbarium that you fill in yourself. It is also what you take away from the game.

🗣️

Cebuano first

Names, riddles and sayings are in Cebuano, with English help underneath. Saying the right name is what solves the puzzle.

🌙

Stories of the place

Folklore is treated with respect, as stories told by people, not as monsters to fight. Gentle wonder, never horror.

🍃

Calm and kind

No timers, no game over. Wrong guesses get gentle hints, so the player can't get stuck.

2. Story frame: Lola Almira

Lola Almira is a botanist who has spent her life in her seaside garden. For every flower she grows, she writes a page in her journal: a poem and everything she knows about it, its name, when it blooms, how the old folk used it, and the stories told about it.

Now Lola is going blind. The handwriting in the journal gets shakier, the drawings stop, and the last pages are only half written. She can still smell the dama de noche opening at night and knows each leaf by touch, but she can no longer find her flowers or write them down.

Ikaw, her apo, comes home to stay with her. Lola gives Ikaw the journal and asks for help finishing it: "Ikaw na ang akong mata." (You will be my eyes.)

For each flower, Ikaw follows the clues, finds the plant and describes it to Lola. When Lola recognises it, she recites its poem and tells what she knows, and Ikaw writes it down. Ikaw's handwriting appears next to Lola's on the page.

When all 18 pages are complete, the garden awakens (the existing finale). Lola's knowledge has been passed on and is no longer only in her memory.

Based on the premise of Erlinda K. Alburo's Sinug-ang Hardin ni Almira as described to us (Almira, a botanist going blind, with a poem and knowledge for each flower). Her actual poems will only be used with her permission. Until then, the pages carry Lola's plain spoken knowledge, and we will not write new poems in her name.

Why this fits the SP so well

Lola's fading sight is the same problem the SP is about: folk knowledge that lives in one elder's memory and is lost when no one learns it. The player doesn't just read the knowledge. They take part in passing it on from one generation to the next.

The fading journal: clues change as Lola's sight fades

The journal itself tells the story. The order of the chapters follows how Lola's pages were written, from when she could still see to after she couldn't. That changes how the player has to find each flower.

By the night chapter, even Ikaw can barely see, so the player finds flowers the way Lola does: by scent, sound and memory. The game teaches empathy without needing to explain it.

Key mechanic: Ihulagway kay Lola (describe it to Lola)

This replaces simply clicking to collect, and it's what makes the game its own. After finding a flower, Ikaw goes back to Lola (on the papag, the duyan or by the window) and describes it:

  1. The player picks 3 details they observed from their notes: colour, shape or petal count, scent, when it opens, where it grows, how the leaf feels.
  2. Right details: Lola smiles, says its Cebuano name and recites its poem and knowledge. The card appears and the page is stamped.
  3. Unclear details: Lola asks a question back ("Does it smell at night, day?"). That is a gentle hint, never a failure.
  4. Some flowers Ikaw brings to Lola to touch or smell, like the waxy rosal or the sampaguita garland.

Possible extra: hold a key (e.g. O, which is free; H already hides the UI) for "Mata ni Lola", a short moment where the view goes soft and dim and the scents and sounds of the garden get stronger, so the player can feel how Lola finds her flowers. This should be done gently and checked with people who have low vision, so it's respectful and not a gimmick.

3. Core loop: the Botany Manor style deduction

In Botany Manor, you are given a plant you can't identify yet. You collect clues from letters, books and the house, then confirm what the plant needs before it blooms. We adapt that loop to Cebuano plants, folk knowledge and Lola's unfinished pages:

Clue types (each flower has 3–4)

ClueWhere it comes fromWhat it teaches
Lola's pageWhat Lola managed to write: a few lines, a drawing or a pressed petal. Plus things she says while you sit with her.Memory, emotion, how the plant was used at home
TigmoA Cebuano riddle in the margin, with English helpLanguage: the Cebuano name and descriptive words

NeighbourA villager's story or request (a cough, a fever, a fiesta)Folk use and the community around the plant
SugilanonA folk story told at dusk or nightFolklore and beliefs attached to the plant or place

The deduction itself

Each journal page has three blanks. The player fills them by picking from words they have already collected (a word bank that grows as clues are found):

  1. Ngalan (name): the Cebuano name, e.g. manul, antulunga, balunsaying.
  2. Kanus-a / asa (when or where): blooms at night, near water, climbs the fence, grows by the churchyard.
  3. Gamit (use): fever tea, poultice for boils, garland for the altar, dye, "do not eat, poison".

The blanks are the parts Lola could no longer see to write. With the blanks filled in, the player finds the living plant, collects it into the bayong (the existing click and bayong flow) and describes it to Lola. Her answer, the poem and the card, is the reward.

5. Folklore layer: Sugilanon sa Sugbo

Short stories told by Lola, a neighbour or a fisherman, each tied to a place or plant in the garden. They give the garden a soul and teach the beliefs behind everyday habits. All of these need checking with Ma'am Gabales, Ma'am Alburo or local elders before writing the final text, and should name the town or source.

6. Game flow

One day in the garden, split by the time of day the game already has (Buntag → Udto → Hapon → Gabii). Each chapter opens part of the garden and adds a new type of clue.

Prologue · tutorial

Sumpa kita

  • Ikaw arrives at the gate. Lola is on the papag and can't quite see Ikaw's face. She hands over the journal: "Ikaw na ang akong mata."
  • Flower: sampaguita (manul). Teaches the whole loop with only two blanks: clue → deduce → collect → describe to Lola → page stamped.
  • Payoff: Lola threads a sampaguita garland by touch, and Ikaw promises to finish the journal (sumpa kita, "I promise you").
Chapter I · Buntag

Ang nataran (the yard)

  • Flowers: gumamela, santan, rosas, bogambilya, pukingan.
  • Early, clear pages: Lola's drawings and colour notes. New clue type: tigmo riddles. Morning chores (well, hens) reveal sensory clues.
  • Example: gumamela. Children blow bubbles from its crushed petals; Nang Iday asks for it for a boil. Gamit = poultice.
  • Folklore: Flores de Mayo memory on the rosas page.
Chapter II · Udto

Ang lim-aw ug baybayon (pond and shore)

  • Flowers: liryo, canna (balunsaying), heliconia, orkidyas, kadena.
  • Middle pages: the drawings stop, the handwriting grows. New clue type: neighbour stories. The fisherman at the bangka, a child by the pond.
  • Example: canna. "A drink to cool the body after working in the sun" plus "seeds hard as shot", which children use as beads (kolintasan).
  • Folklore: Maria Cacao, told at the shore as the afternoon turns.
Chapter III · Hapon

Tambal (remedies)

  • Flowers: kamia, ilang-ilang, tsampaka, adelfa.
  • Pages written by touch and scent. New mechanic: remedy pages, where Lola teaches by smell and feel. A visitor describes a problem; the player picks plants from the journal (for example kamia for a sore-throat gargle, or tuob steam).
  • Adelfa is the lesson on caution: beautiful but poisonous, used only on the skin in folk practice. Choosing it for a tea gives a gentle warning and the "never eat" fact.
  • Folklore: mananambal, tuob, buyag.
Chapter IV · Gabii

Mga sugilanon (night stories)

  • Flowers: dama de noche, kalachuchi, rosal. Lola's last pages are almost blank. Ikaw finds the flowers by scent, the way Lola does now.
  • New clue type: sugilanon at the shrine. The "tabi" moment at the old tree. Fireflies only at night (as now).
  • Folklore: tamawo and the dalakit, kalag-kalag, Holy Week herb gathering.
Epilogue

Awakening

  • 18/18 pages: Ikaw reads the whole journal aloud to Lola under the stars, and the existing awaken magic plays. Her final page, written together, is about passing the garden on.
  • The completed journal can be saved or printed as a herbarium, which is the player's takeaway and a good thesis artifact.

8. Scope for the SP

Must have
  • Lola Almira (character + voice lines)
  • "Describe to Lola" step
  • Journal with 18 pages and blanks
  • Clue collection and word bank
  • Deduce → collect → stamp loop
  • Prologue + 4 chapters
  • Ending
Nice to have
  • Tigmo for every flower
  • 3–4 neighbour NPCs
  • Remedy pages
  • "Tabi" mechanic
  • Recorded Cebuano voice lines
Stretch
  • Lines from Alburo's poems (with permission)
  • Printable herbarium
  • Small hands-on mini-games (garland threading)

Evaluation ideas

  • Learning: a short quiz before and after playing (name, use and caution for 6–8 flowers).
  • Experience: a game experience questionnaire or the System Usability Scale (SUS), plus a few open questions about what they remember.
  • Cultural accuracy: a review of the content by Ma'am Gabales, Ma'am Alburo (if willing) or a local elder or mananambal.
  • Performance: frame rate on a regular school laptop (the existing ?perf=1 tool).

9. Open questions

  1. How closely should the game follow Ma'am Alburo's story of Almira, and may we use her poems on each page?
  2. Target players: high school students, college students or general?
  3. How much English help: always shown, or a toggle key?
  4. Which folklore stories can we confirm with a source or a person, and from which town?
  5. Who checks the Cebuano lines (riddles, notes)? Ma'am Gabales' theater students could also record the voices.

Folk uses are from public Philippine ethnobotany sources (StuartXchange, PTKDL) and are not medical advice. Flowers are typical Cebuano garden plants, not taken from Alburo's text.
